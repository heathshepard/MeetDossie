### 2. Conventional Fannie/Freddie investment financing — blocked twice over

**The LLC is a hard procedural gate, verified.** Fannie Selling Guide B2-1.3-03: *"In order to
close the refinance transaction, ownership must be transferred out of the LLC and into the name
of the individual borrower(s)."* B2-1.3-02 repeats it for limited cash-out. B2-2-01 confirms
borrowers must generally be natural persons, with no LLC exception. Time held by the LLC does
count toward seasoning where the borrower majority-owns it.

**Deeding back into the LLC after closing was NOT verified.** Loan officers commonly say it's
fine; no Selling Guide language confirming it was found. Absence of a prohibition is not
permission. Get it in writing.

**And here is the flag that should not be glossed:** 12 U.S.C. §1701j-3 (Garn-St Germain)
generally *permits* lenders to enforce due-on-sale. Its subsection (d) exemptions are narrow —
death, spouse/children, divorce decree, inter vivos trust — and while they apply to residential
property of fewer than five dwelling units, **there is no exemption for an LLC↔individual
transfer.** Deeding 702 Fawndale out of Riperian is therefore a transfer the existing 8.375%
lender could treat as triggering due-on-sale. That risk is **real, not hypothetical**, and it
exists the moment the deed moves — before any new loan is approved. *(Statutory text verified
via Cornell LII, pulled 2026-09-19. This is a reading of the statute, not a legal opinion —
Josh Sisam should confirm before any deed is recorded.)*

**LTV:** Selling Guide B2-1.3-03 defers to the Eligibility Matrix, which returned 403 and could
not be read. Investment 1-unit cash-out is conventionally capped around 75%, limited cash-out
75–80% — **inferred, not verified this session.** At 84–88% he fails either way.

**Texas transfer tax:** almost certainly none (Texas has no real property transfer tax), but not
confirmed against the Tax Code this session. Title re-issue and loss of the liability shield
while title sits in his personal name are real costs of the manoeuvre.

**One more thing nobody mentions:** conventional underwriting qualifies *him*, not the property.
A self-employed REALTOR carrying a rental that loses $785–1,237/mo would have that negative
cash flow charged against his DTI. **Inferred from standard agency practice, not verified.**

### 4. VA — plainly, no. It does not help him here.

**VA-guaranteed financing cannot be used on a non-owner-occupied rental.** VA's own cash-out
page states eligibility requires that *"You'll live in the home you're refinancing with the

His 100% disability rating **waives the VA funding fee** (well-established; not re-sourced this

The one legitimate VA play: a **VA cash-out refinance on the home he actually occupies**

## OPTIONS THAT DON'T REQUIRE A REFINANCE

### Recast — doesn't solve the problem
A recast re-amortizes the existing note after a large principal payment. **It does not change
the rate.** He'd stay at 8.375%. Rough math: a $30,000 paydown recast on the current note drops
P&I by roughly $230/mo — worse than the refinance scenarios, for comparable cash. And recast is
a lender courtesy, not a right: it is standard on agency loans and **uncommon on non-QM,
business-purpose paper** like this one. Whether SPS/Fay even offers it on this note is unknown
and would have to be asked. **Not verified — no recast policy for this loan was located.**

### Loan modification — not available, and not wanted
Modifications are loss-mitigation tools for borrowers in or near default. The loan is performing.
Asking for one on a performing business-purpose loan is not a product that exists, and pursuing
it would mean creating the hardship first. **Do not go down this road.**

### Assumption — worthless here, and backwards
An assumable 8.375% note is not a selling feature when the market is at 7.20%. A buyer gains
nothing by assuming an above-market rate. (Whether the note is even assumable was not verified —
but it doesn't matter, because the direction of the rate gap makes it a non-asset.)

### Rate buydown — this is a SALE tool, and it's the one worth costing
A seller-paid 2-1 temporary buydown or permanent points typically costs 2–3% of the buyer's loan
and can move a hesitant buyer more than an equivalent price cut. On a $330,000 sale that's
roughly $6,000–9,000 of concession. **This is the only financing lever on the list that actually
helps him, and it helps him sell — not hold.** Worth modelling against a straight price
reduction if the listing stalls; 622 Sunhaven (the closest renovated no-pool comp) has sat 61
days at $339,900, so stalling is the live risk.

### HELOC / borrowing against other equity — solves nothing, adds risk
Pulling equity from his Boerne homestead to prop up Fawndale means **borrowing at 8–10% to fund
an asset that loses $1,237/mo**. That is doubling down on the losing position.

Texas-specific constraints he should know before anyone suggests it (**general knowledge of
Texas homestead law, NOT re-verified from a 2026 citation — confirm with Josh Sisam**):
Texas Constitution Art. XVI §50(a)(6) caps total homestead-secured debt at **80% of fair market
value**, limits fees, imposes a 12-day cooling-off period, allows only one such loan per
12 months, and — critically — once a §50(a)(6) home-equity loan exists against the homestead it
permanently carries that character. These protections apply **only to the homestead**. They do
not apply to Fawndale, which is non-owner-occupied business-purpose collateral.

### Do nothing and re-lease
Keep the 8.375% note, re-tenant at market, and accept **−$1,237/mo fully loaded**
(−$785/mo on contractual bills alone) at the verified $2,300 market rent. This is the honest
"hold" baseline and it is what the refinance scenarios must beat. They beat it by $79–467/mo —
never by enough to justify $17k–41k of new cash into a depreciating asset, and never by enough
to reach positive cash flow.

Note this is meaningfully worse than the $1,044/mo the 2026-09-10 analysis carried, purely
because that report used the last achieved rent of $2,550 rather than today's market. **The hold
case in that report is optimistic by roughly $193/mo, or $11,600 over five years.**
