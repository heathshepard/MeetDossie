## 5. WHAT CHANGED FROM 20-18 — 4 to 5 minutes

> **This is the segment that makes the video rank and get shared. Every claim below was
> verified against the official TREC redline. Nothing here is guessed.**

`[SCREEN: split — 20-18 on the left, 20-19 on the right, or just scroll the redline PDF]`

### Change 1 — new ¶7.I, the Seller's Water Disclosure

> Biggest one first.
> There's a new paragraph 7-I.
> It did not exist in 20-18 at all.

> Seller's Disclosure About Groundwater and Surface Water Rights.
> Check one box only.
> Buyer received it, Buyer hasn't received it, or Seller isn't required to deliver it.

> Read that middle box.
> If the Buyer never receives it,
> the Buyer can terminate any time before closing and get the earnest money back.

> And if the Seller does deliver it late,
> the Buyer gets seven days from receipt to terminate for any reason.
> Any reason. Earnest money refunded.

`[FACE]`

> That is a live termination right sitting in the middle of your contract,
> and it's brand new,
> and on most urban resales nobody's thinking about water rights.

`[SCREEN: ¶7.I(3), the five sub-conditions (a) through (e)]`

> Box three is the one you'll check most in town, and it has conditions.
> All five have to be true.
> No water well. No pond, lake or water tank.

> No certificate of adjudication, certified filing, or surface water permit with TCEQ.
> No groundwater rights severed, sold, or leased.
> And you name the water provider — city, MUD, water supply corporation, or private company.

`[FACE]`

> Here's the practitioner note.
> In a platted San Antonio subdivision on SAWS, checking box three takes ten seconds.
> Out in the Hill Country, on acreage, with a well — that is not a box, that's a conversation.

> And "Seller is not aware of a water well, in use or not in use" —
> not in use.
> A capped, forgotten well in the back corner still counts.

### Change 2 — brokerage compensation moved and became two-way

`[SCREEN: redline of ¶12, showing the struck 12A(1)(b) and the new 12B]`

> In 20-18, seller contribution toward the buyer's broker lived in paragraph 12-A-1-b.
> It's gone from there.
> There is now a whole paragraph 12-B called Brokerage Compensation.

> And it runs both ways.
> 12-B-1: Seller will pay a dollar amount or a percent toward the compensation the Buyer owes the Buyer's broker.
> 12-B-2: Buyer will pay a dollar amount or a percent toward what the Seller owes the Seller's broker.

> Both are check-one-box-only between dollars and percent.
> Both have their own checkbox to turn the whole line on.
> That's three boxes each if you count the enabling box.

> Read the bolded sentence above them.
> These contributions apply to, and do not change,
> what each party already owes their own broker under their separate written agreement.

`[FACE]`

> That language matters.
> A seller contribution is not a commission agreement.
> If your buyer rep agreement says two and a half and the seller contributes two,
> your client owes the other half. Say that out loud at the table.

> Also gone: the old 20-18 paragraph 12-B that let a party terminate
> if an expense exceeded a stated amount.
> Struck. It's now paragraph 12-C, Expense Limitation, and it does something narrower.

### Change 3 — ¶8 renamed, and brokers' fees deleted from it

`[SCREEN: ¶8]`

> Paragraph 8 used to be "Brokers and Sales Agents" with an A and a B.
> It's now "Broker or Sales Agent Disclosure," single block.
> The old 8-B — "all obligations for payment of brokers' fees are in separate written agreements" — is deleted.

> What's left is the licensee-as-a-party disclosure,
> and it's broader than people think.
> You, your spouse, your parent, your child.

> A business entity you own more than ten percent of.
> A trust you're trustee of, or that you, your spouse, parent or child benefit from.
> In writing, before entering into a contract of sale.

### Change 4 — ¶6.E lost item (12); notices moved to ¶22

`[SCREEN: ¶6.E ending at (11); then ¶22 statutory disclosures group]`

> Title notices in paragraph 6-E used to run to twelve.
> Now they stop at eleven, at the mold remediation certificate.
> Old (12), "Required Notices," is deleted.

> It moved into paragraph 22, under a new heading, "Statutory Disclosures and Notices."
> There's a line there to list all the utility, water, drainage, public improvement and other district notices.

> And a bolded warning under it:
> Seller's failure to provide applicable statutory disclosures and notices
> may give Buyer remedies or rights to terminate.

> Same trap, new address.
> If you've been checking the old 6-E-12 box out of habit, it isn't there anymore.

### Change 5 — ¶22 addenda list reorganized into five groups

`[SCREEN: ¶22, page 9]`

> The addenda checklist is no longer one long column.
> Five groups: Financial. Leases. Additional Tests and Reports.
> Statutory Disclosures and Notices. Other.

> Nothing to memorize, but if you're used to finding a box by position on the page,
> stop. Read the group headers.

### Change 6 — ¶20, and the small cleanups

`[SCREEN: ¶20 and ¶21]`

> Paragraph 20 was Federal Requirements. It's now Governmental Requirements,
> and there's a new 20-B: both parties promptly execute and deliver whatever Escrow Agent needs
> for governmental reporting. Treasury and its bureaus, named.

> Paragraph 21, notices: fax is gone as a delivery method.
> Mail, hand delivery, overnight courier, or electronic transmission.

> Paragraph 17, attorney's fees: "Listing Broker" and "Other Broker"
> are now "Seller's broker" and "Buyer's broker."

> And on the broker page, the old disclosure line —
> "pursuant to a previous separate agreement, Listing Broker has agreed to pay Other Broker a fee" —
> is deleted. That's the post-settlement cleanup finishing.

`[FACE]`

> That's the delta. Now the whole form, in order.

## 6. THE WALKTHROUGH

> **Convention:** `[SCREEN: ...]` = document on screen, his face off or inset.
> `[FACE]` = cut to him. Every `[FACE]` beat is a place where his eyes leave the script
> anyway, so the edit has cover on both sides.

### ¶1 — PARTIES

`[SCREEN: page 1, ¶1]`

> Two blanks. Seller, Buyer.
> Legal names. Not "Bob," not "the Smiths," not the LLC's DBA.
> Whatever's on the deed and whatever's going on the deed.

> If title's held by a trust, the trust and the trustee go here.
> If your seller is married and it's homestead, both spouses sign even if one's not on the deed.
> That's Family Code, not TREC, and it will stop your closing.

### ¶2 — PROPERTY

`[SCREEN: ¶2.A through 2.E]`

> 2-A, the land. Lot, block, addition, city, county, Texas, then the address and zip.
> Or "as described on attached exhibit."
> The legal description controls. The address is a convenience.

> 2-B, improvements. Read this list once in your life.
> Ceiling fans, mounts and brackets for televisions and speakers, water softener system,
> garage door openers, shrubbery, landscaping, outdoor cooking equipment, generators.

`[FACE]`

> Generators are in there. Mounted TV brackets are in there.
> If the seller is taking the bracket, that goes in 2-D exclusions, in writing,
> before it turns into a walkthrough argument over a hundred-dollar piece of steel.

`[SCREEN: ¶2.C, zoom on the "Controls" sentence]`

> 2-C, accessories. Window units, stove, curtains and rods, door keys, mailbox keys,
> above-ground pool, security systems that are not fixtures.

> And controls. Garage doors, entry gates, other improvements and accessories.
> Then it defines Controls: the seller's transferable rights to the software and apps
> used to access and control the thing, and the hardware used solely to control it.

> So the app that opens the gate conveys.
> Which is why paragraph 10-B exists, and we'll get there.

> 2-D, exclusions. What the seller keeps and must remove before possession.
> Free text. Be specific. "Curtains" is not specific in a house with fourteen windows.

> 2-E, reservations. Any reservation of oil, gas, other minerals, water, timber,
> is made by attached addendum.
> No addendum, no reservation. It's one sentence and it's absolute.

### ¶3 — SALES PRICE

`[SCREEN: ¶3.A, B, C]`

> Three lines. A plus B equals C.
> A is the cash portion. B is the financed portion. C is the sales price.

> Read the sentence under A.
> Cash portion does not include proceeds from borrowing of any kind,
> or from selling other real property, except as disclosed in this contract.

`[FACE]`

> That's the "cash offer" that's actually a HELOC.
> If your buyer is borrowing against something else to make the cash side work,
> that's a disclosure, and if it's not disclosed you have a problem later.

`[SCREEN: ¶3.B checkboxes]`

> 3-B has three checkboxes.
> Third Party Financing Addendum. Loan Assumption Addendum. Seller Financing Addendum.
> Check the box and attach the addendum. Both. Every time.

> A financed number in 3-B with no addendum attached
> means your buyer has no financing contingency.
> It means it. That's not a technicality.

### ¶4 — LEASES

`[SCREEN: ¶4 intro paragraph, then A, B, C]`

> First read the lead-in, because everyone skips to the boxes.
> After the effective date, Seller may not, without Buyer's written consent,
> create a new lease, amend an existing lease, or convey any interest in the Property.

> Then: check all applicable boxes. All, not one.

> 4-A, residential leases. Tenant in place, addendum attached.
> 4-B, fixture leases. Their examples: solar panels, propane tanks, water softener, security system.
> 4-C, natural resource leases. Oil and gas, mineral, geothermal, water, wind.

`[SCREEN: ¶4.C(1) and (2)]`

> 4-C has two sub-boxes and they're a pair.
> One: Seller has delivered copies of all the leases.
> Two: Seller has not, and will within three days after the effective date.

> And then a blank: Buyer may terminate within blank days after receiving them.
> Earnest money refunded.

`[FACE]`

> Solar is the one that bites in San Antonio.
> A leased system is a fixture lease under 4-B,
> and the assumption paperwork from the solar company takes longer than your option period.

> If there are panels on the roof, 4-B gets checked on day one,
> and you start the transfer application the same day.
> Not after the inspection.

### ¶5 — EARNEST MONEY AND TERMINATION OPTION

> **The highest-value paragraph in the form. Slow down here.**

`[SCREEN: ¶5.A, zoom on the escrow agent blank and the two dollar blanks]`

> 5-A. Within three days after the effective date,
> Buyer must deliver to — blank, the escrow agent, at — blank, the address —
> dollar blank as earnest money, and dollar blank as the option fee.

> Both payable to Escrow Agent.
> May be paid separately or combined in a single payment.
> That's the 20-18 change that's still catching people: one check to title, not two, not one to the seller.

> 5-A-1, additional earnest money. A dollar amount, within blank days.
> Usually left empty. Used when you're stepping up earnest money after the option period.

`[SCREEN: ¶5.A(2)]`

> 5-A-2 is the weekend rule and you need to know it cold.
> If the last day to deliver the earnest money, option fee, or additional earnest money
> falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or Legal Holiday,
> it extends to the end of the next day that isn't one of those.

> And Legal Holiday is defined — Government Code 662.003(a), and (b)(4) and (6).
> Not "whatever the title company calls a holiday."

`[FACE — this is the war story beat, see §11 for the guardrails]`

> Here's why I care about this one.

> I had a file where the earnest money went in three days late.
> Three days.
> And the consequence wasn't a fee — the buyer lost the ability to terminate under the option.

> The setup is the part nobody sees coming.
> You execute on a Wednesday or a Thursday.
> Your three-day window runs straight into a weekend, into a title company that is closed.

> Monday morning you are already late,
> and the option period is burning the entire time.
> Five thousand two hundred dollars, on one calendar mistake.

> So: if you execute Wednesday through Friday,
> deal with it at execution. Not Monday.
> Amendment, extension, or wired funds that day.

`[SCREEN: ¶5.A(3) and (4)]`

> 5-A-3, the application order. Escrow applies what it receives
> first to the option fee, then to earnest money, then to additional earnest money.
> So a short payment shorts the earnest money, not the option fee.

> 5-A-4. Buyer authorizes Escrow Agent to release the option fee to Seller at any time,
> no notice, no consent, and releases escrow from liability for doing it.
> The option fee is credited to the sales price at closing.

`[SCREEN: ¶5.B]`

> 5-B, the termination option itself.
> Unrestricted right to terminate by giving notice to Seller within blank days after the effective date.
> That's your option period.

> Notices under this paragraph must be given by five p.m.
> local time where the Property is located.
> Not where your buyer is. Where the house is.

> If they terminate in time: the option fee is not refunded,
> escrow releases anything remaining to the Seller,
> and the earnest money is refunded to Buyer.

`[FACE]`

> On waiving the option period, because somebody always asks.
> For a normal retail buyer in a multiple-offer situation, I don't advise it.
> You're not buying leverage, you're moving risk onto your client.

> Where it's defensible: a cash investor doing a gut renovation who's already priced the risk,
> or a contractor who can walk the house and tell you what's wrong in twenty minutes.
> Those people can price uncertainty. A first-time buyer cannot.

> And a shorter option at a higher fee is usually the better trade than no option at all.

`[SCREEN: ¶5.C, D, E]`

> 5-C. Buyer fails to deliver earnest money on time —
> Seller may terminate, or use paragraph 15 remedies, or both.
> But read the last clause: by notice to Buyer *before* Buyer delivers the earnest money.

> So it's a window, not a permanent right.
> Buyer wires it late on day five and the seller said nothing?
> That remedy's gone.

> 5-D. If no dollar amount is stated as the option fee,
> or the Buyer doesn't deliver it on time,
> the Buyer does not have the unrestricted right to terminate.

> A blank option fee is not a free option. It's no option.

> 5-E. Time is of the essence for this paragraph,
> and strict compliance with the time for performance is required.
> That sentence is the whole reason this paragraph is dangerous.

### ¶6 — TITLE POLICY AND SURVEY

`[SCREEN: ¶6.A]`

> 6-A. Seller furnishes the owner's title policy at — checkbox — Seller's or Buyer's expense.
> Title company blank. Amount of the sales price. Dated at or after closing.
> Then nine permitted exceptions.

> Skim one through seven and nine. Stop on eight.

`[SCREEN: zoom hard on ¶6.A(8)(i) and (ii)]`

> Eight is the standard printed exception for discrepancies, conflicts,
> shortages in area or boundary lines, encroachments, protrusions, overlapping improvements.
> And you pick one of two boxes.

> (i) will not be amended or deleted from the title policy. Or —
> (ii) will be amended to read "shortages in area," at the expense of — Buyer or Seller.

`[FACE]`

> This gets asked on every version of this video, so here's the practical answer.

> Amending it to read "shortages in area" narrows the exception.
> You're buying coverage for boundary and encroachment problems,
> keeping only the acreage shortfall carved out.

> It is not free, and it is not automatic.
> The title company will want a survey and a T-47 before they'll do it.
> Which means box (ii) and your paragraph 6-C survey election have to agree with each other.

> If the seller's paying, ask — many sellers will, it's small money.
> If nobody's paying and you're on a clean recent survey in a platted subdivision,
> you can live without it.

> On acreage, on anything with a fence line that isn't the property line,
> on anything where a neighbor's shed might be over — I want it amended.
> That's where the claims come from.

`[SCREEN: ¶6.B]`

> 6-B, the commitment.
> Within twenty days after the Title Company receives a copy of this contract —
> note that, it runs from title's receipt, not the effective date —
> Seller furnishes the commitment, and at Buyer's expense the exception documents.

> If it's late, the time automatically extends up to fifteen days,
> or three days before closing, whichever is earlier.
> If it still doesn't come, Buyer may terminate and the earnest money is refunded.

`[FACE]`

> The practical version: get the contract to title the day it's executed.
> Every day you sit on it is a day off the front of that twenty.

`[SCREEN: ¶6.C, all three options]`

> 6-C, survey. And it says check one box only.
> One. Not "whichever seems right." One.

> Option one: within blank days, Seller furnishes the existing survey
> plus a T-47 affidavit or a T-47.1 declaration.
> If Seller fails to furnish both, Buyer obtains a new survey at *Seller's* expense,
> no later than three days before closing.

> Then a second checkbox inside option one:
> if the title company or the buyer's lender won't accept the existing survey or the affidavit,
> Buyer gets a new one at — Seller's or Buyer's expense. You pick.

> Option two: within blank days Buyer may obtain a new survey at Buyer's expense.
> And read the sentence after it —
> if Buyer fails to obtain the survey, Buyer may not terminate
> under paragraph 2-B of the Third Party Financing Addendum because the survey wasn't obtained.

> Option three: within blank days, Seller at Seller's expense furnishes a new survey.

`[FACE]`

> Two things I'd tell a newer agent.

> One — option one has a hidden cost shift. If your seller says "I've got a survey"
> and then can't produce the T-47 in time, your seller just bought a new survey.
> Get eyes on both documents before you check that box.

> Two — that inner Seller-or-Buyer expense box inside option one gets left blank constantly.
> It's a check-one-box-only field with money attached, and an unchecked box is an argument.

`[SCREEN: ¶6.D]`

> 6-D, objections.
> Buyer may object in writing to defects, exceptions or encumbrances
> disclosed on the survey other than 6-A items one through seven,
> disclosed in the commitment other than 6-A items one through nine,
> or which prohibit a use or activity — and there's a blank to write that use in.

> Timing: the earlier of the closing date,
> or blank days after Buyer receives the commitment, the exception documents, *and* the survey.
> All three. The clock doesn't start until the last one lands.

> Failure to object in time is a waiver —
> except the Schedule C requirements, which are never waived.

> Seller isn't obligated to spend money, but shall cure timely objections
> within fifteen days. That's the Cure Period, and closing extends as needed.

> Not cured? Buyer has five days after the Cure Period ends to either
> terminate with the earnest money refunded, or waive.
> Do nothing and you're deemed to have waived.

> And if the commitment or survey gets revised, or a new exception document shows up,
> the same objection window restarts for the new matter only.

`[SCREEN: ¶6.E, scroll (1) through (11)]`

> 6-E, title notices. Eleven of them now. They're notices, not blanks — except one.

> One, abstract or title policy.
> Two, property owners association — and this one has an *is / is not* checkbox. Fill it.
> Three, statutory tax districts. Four, tide waters. Five, annexation and ETJ.

> Six, certificated service area of a utility provider.
> Seven, public improvement districts. Eight, transfer fees.
> Nine, propane gas system service area. Ten, water level fluctuations.
> Eleven, certificate of mold remediation — five years back.

`[FACE]`

> 6-E-2, the POA box. Mandatory membership, is or is not.
> In most of Bexar County the answer is "is,"
> and checking it is what tells the title company to order the resale certificate.

> An unchecked POA box is how you find out about a two-thousand-dollar transfer fee
> forty-eight hours before closing.

> And PID — number seven — matters more here than most places.
> New construction sections around Loop 1604, a lot of them are in one.
> It rides on the tax bill and it surprises buyers.

### ¶7 — PROPERTY CONDITION

`[SCREEN: ¶7.A]`

> 7-A, access, inspections and utilities.
> Seller permits access at reasonable times. Buyer's inspectors must be TREC-licensed
> or otherwise permitted by law.
