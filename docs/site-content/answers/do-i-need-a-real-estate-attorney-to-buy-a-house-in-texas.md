# Do I need a real estate attorney to buy a house in Texas?

No. Texas does not require a real estate attorney to buy a house. The title company handles escrow, title search and closing documents, and license holders use TREC promulgated forms. An attorney is the right call for title defects, probate or estate sales, deed drafting, deed restriction questions, and any custom contract language.

## The contract itself tells you to hire one

Before anything else: the promulgated contract does not treat a lawyer as optional advice from a website. It says so on its own face, twice.

**Paragraph 23 is titled CONSULT AN ATTORNEY BEFORE SIGNING.** Its text: "**TREC rules prohibit real estate brokers and sales agents from giving legal advice. READ THIS CONTRACT CAREFULLY.**" The form then provides printed fields for Buyer's Attorney and Seller's Attorney, with phone numbers ([TREC form 20-19, ¶23](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf)).

**Paragraph 6E(1)** goes further and names the specific job: "**Broker advises Buyer to have an abstract of title covering the Property examined by an attorney of Buyer's selection**, or Buyer should be furnished with or obtain a Title Policy. If a Title Policy is furnished, the **Commitment should be promptly reviewed by an attorney of Buyer's choice due to the time limitations on Buyer's right to object**."

Read that second one closely. It is not generic caution. It identifies the single highest-value hour a real estate attorney will ever spend on an ordinary residential purchase: reading the title commitment inside the objection window. The contract flags it because that window closes fast and closes permanently.

So the honest answer to "do I need an attorney" is: not to close, and the state built a system so you would not have to. But the contract you are signing has a paragraph whose entire purpose is to tell you to get one, and the reason is title.

## Why Texas works this way

Texas is a title company state. In a residential purchase, the title company acts as **escrow agent**, runs the title search, issues the title commitment and the title policy, prepares the closing documents, and disburses funds at closing. That is the function an attorney performs in the states that require one.

The second half of the system is the TREC promulgated contract. The Texas Real Estate Commission writes the forms, and license holders are required to use them ([TREC contract forms](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts)). A REALTOR is not drafting your contract — they are filling blanks on a form the state wrote.

That is not a convenience. It is the reason the system works without a lawyer in every deal. **Drafting contract language is the practice of law, and a real estate license does not authorize it** — which is exactly what ¶23 means by "TREC rules prohibit real estate brokers and sales agents from giving legal advice." The promulgated forms exist precisely so a license holder can paper a transaction without crossing that line. TREC does not write custom contracts.

So the framing is not "you do not need a lawyer in Texas." It is: the routine case has been pre-lawyered by the state, and the non-routine case still needs one.

## The limits of what your agent can do

A Texas license holder can explain how a promulgated form works, fill in the negotiated terms, and advise on price, condition, market and strategy. That covers most of what a buyer needs.

A license holder cannot draft contract language, interpret a deed restriction for you as a legal matter, advise you on how to take title, tell you what a title exception means for your legal rights, or opine on the effect of a divorce decree or a will on the chain of title.

When a client asks a question that lands past that line, the correct answer is a referral, not a guess. An agent who freelances a special provision to solve a problem is creating a bigger one.

## When an attorney is the right call

These are the situations where paying for an hour of a real estate attorney's time is cheap relative to the exposure.

**A title defect the commitment flagged.** A break in the chain of title, an unreleased lien, a judgment, a probate that was never completed, a missing heir, a boundary discrepancy the survey exposed. The title company will tell you it cannot insure over something. It will not tell you how to fix it.

This is the ¶6E(1) situation, and it is on a clock. Under **¶6D** the buyer must object in writing by the earlier of the closing date or a stated number of days after receiving the commitment, exception documents and survey, and "failure to object within the time allowed **will constitute a waiver** of Buyer's right to object" — with only the Schedule C requirements surviving ([TREC form 20-19, ¶6D](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf)). If the seller does not cure a timely objection within the **15-day cure period**, the buyer gets **5 days** to terminate with earnest money refunded or waive, and silence waives.

A buyer who sends the commitment to a lawyer the week of closing has usually already waived. The hour costs the same either way; only the timing determines whether it was worth anything.

**Probate and estate sales.** An estate selling a property raises questions about the authority of the person signing, whether the will was probated, whether an independent administration exists, and whether all heirs have joined. This is one of the most common reasons a Texas residential deal goes sideways late.

**Deed drafting.** If the transaction needs a deed prepared for something beyond the ordinary conveyance — a transfer into a trust or an entity, a correction deed, a deed with a reserved life estate, a gift deed between family — a lawyer drafts it. Nobody else may.

**Custom contract language.** Any term the promulgated forms and addenda do not accommodate. Seller financing structured beyond the addendum, a leaseback longer than the promulgated temporary lease contemplates, a right of first refusal, an option tied to rezoning or platting, a seller carryback with unusual terms. A license holder may not write these.

**Deed restriction interpretation.** Whether a "residential purposes only" covenant permits a short-term rental, whether a restriction is enforceable after a subdivision amendment, whether a use is grandfathered. Deed restrictions are private contracts, and their interpretation is legal work.

**Partition.** Co-owners who cannot agree on selling, or inherited property split among siblings.

**Marital property.** Texas is a **community property** state. How a married person takes title, whether a non-purchasing spouse must join, what a divorce decree did to the property, and how separate property is characterized are all legal questions with real consequences.

**An entity or trust buying.** The formation, the authority to sign, and the operating documents the title company will demand.

**A 1031 exchange.** The attorney works alongside a CPA and a qualified intermediary. The tax answer is not the real estate agent's to give.

**Any dispute.** A contested earnest money release, an alleged default, a non-disclosure claim after closing, a seller refusing to close. Escrowed funds cannot be released without mutual agreement or a court order, and once the other side is dug in, the next move is legal.

## What an attorney will not change

An attorney will not make the title policy cheaper. Texas title insurance premium rates are **promulgated by the Texas Department of Insurance**, so the premium for a given policy amount is the same at every title company in the state ([TDI](https://tdi.texas.gov/title/titlerates2026.html)).

An attorney will not rewrite a promulgated form into something better for routine terms. The forms are good, and the negotiation happens in the blanks.

An attorney will not sit at the closing table and improve a closing that was already going to work.

## The pieces that already protect you without one

The TREC contract and its addenda build in more protection than most buyers realize.

**Paragraph 5B** gives an unrestricted right to terminate for any reason, which functions as a due diligence window. **Paragraph 6B** returns the earnest money if the title commitment never shows up in time. **Paragraph 6D** gives a title objection and cure process. **Paragraph 7B(2)** lets a buyer who never received the seller's disclosure notice terminate at any time prior to closing, and gives a fresh 7 days after the notice finally arrives ([TREC form 20-19](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf); [Texas Property Code §5.008](https://codes.findlaw.com/tx/property-code/prop-sect-5-008/)). **Paragraph 14** handles casualty loss. **Paragraph 15** sets the default remedies both ways.

Outside the base form, the **Third Party Financing Addendum, form 40-11, effective 01/03/2025** supplies a financing exit, and the **Addendum Concerning Right to Terminate Due to Lender's Appraisal, form 49-1** addresses a low appraisal ([TREC contract forms](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts)).

The current resale contract is **TREC form 20-19, effective 07/01/2026** — 12 pages, footer-dated 05-04-2026 ([TREC](https://www.trec.texas.gov/forms/one-four-family-residential-contract-resale)).

Use those, and the ordinary purchase does not need a lawyer. Ignore them and no lawyer hired at closing will fix it.
