# What does the effective date mean in a Texas real estate contract?

The effective date is the date the last party signs the contract and that signature is delivered. It is day zero. Every other deadline in a Texas contract counts from it: the option fee delivery, the option period, the financing deadline, title objections and the closing date. A wrong effective date makes every one of them wrong.

## When the effective date happens

Two events have to occur, in order:

1. **The last party signs.** If the buyer signs Tuesday and the seller signs Thursday, Tuesday is irrelevant to the effective date.
2. **That final signature is delivered** to the other party, in the way the contract provides for delivery.

Signature plus delivery. Not acceptance in principle, not a verbal yes, not "we have a deal" over the phone, not the seller's agent saying the seller is signing tonight.

Things that are **not** the effective date and are regularly mistaken for it:

- The date the buyer wrote the offer.
- The date the seller verbally accepted.
- The date the offer was emailed.
- The date the title company opened the file.
- The date the earnest money was wired.

The effective date is a blank on the contract, filled in when both conditions are met. Under Texas real estate practice it is normally filled in and dated by the broker or agent who receives that last delivery, and it is the one blank on the form that should never be guessed at.

## Why it is the most load-bearing date in the deal

The TREC contract expresses almost nothing as an absolute calendar date. It expresses obligations as **a number of days after the effective date**. Move the effective date by one day and the entire chain slides.

What hangs off it, with the paragraph of the One to Four Family Residential Contract (Resale) that puts it there ([TREC form 20-19, PDF](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf)):

- **¶5A — earnest money and option fee.** "**Within 3 days after the Effective Date**, Buyer must deliver to [Escrow Agent] ... as earnest money and ... as the option fee." They "**may be paid separately or combined in a single payment**." Delivery is to the escrow agent — the title company, not the seller ([TREC](https://www.trec.texas.gov/article/changes-delivery-option-fee-0)).
- **¶5A(1) — additional earnest money**, delivered within a stated number of days after the Effective Date.
- **¶5B — the option period.** The seller grants the buyer "the **unrestricted right to terminate** this contract by giving notice of termination to Seller within [ ] days after the Effective Date of this contract (Option Period)," with notices due "**by 5:00 p.m. (local time where the Property is located)**." Counted in calendar days with the effective date as day zero — a ten-day option period on a November 1 effective date runs out at 5:00 p.m. on November 11 ([Texas Real Estate Research Center](https://trerc.tamu.edu/article/option-period-basics-2360/)).
- **¶6C — the survey.** Whichever of the three survey boxes is checked, it runs on a stated number of days, and two of the three outcomes back up against "**no later than 3 days prior to Closing Date**."
- **¶7B — the seller's disclosure notice**, delivered within a stated number of days after the Effective Date when box (2) is checked.
- **The financing deadline** under the **Third Party Financing Addendum, form 40-11, effective 01/03/2025**. The contract cross-references it in ¶6C(2): a buyer who elects to obtain the survey and fails to "**may not terminate** the contract under Paragraph 2B of the Third Party Financing Addendum because the survey was not obtained" ([TREC contract forms](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts)).
- **¶6B and ¶6D — the title commitment and the objection window.** 6B runs 20 days from when the **Title Company receives a copy of the contract**, which is a different trigger — and a file that does not get opened on day one has already burned days.
- **HOA subdivision information**, where the association has up to 10 business days from a written request, which itself has to start early.
- **Closing**, stated as a date but negotiated against that whole chain.

One date, nine or more downstream obligations. It is the closest thing a Texas contract has to a single point of failure.

## What the contract says about missing those dates

Three sentences from Paragraph 5 decide most disputes about timing.

**¶5D — no option fee, no option.** "If **no dollar amount is stated** as the option fee or if Buyer fails to deliver the option fee within the time required, **Buyer shall not have the unrestricted right to terminate** this contract under this paragraph 5." A blank and a late delivery produce the same result.

**¶5C — late earnest money.** The seller may terminate or exercise remedies under Paragraph 15, or both, by giving notice to the buyer **before the buyer delivers the earnest money**. The window closes when the money lands, which is why a seller who wants to act on it has to act quickly.

**¶5E — time.** "**Time is of the essence** for this paragraph and strict compliance with the time for performance is required." That is the whole doctrine in one line. There is no substantial-compliance argument built into Paragraph 5.

Two mechanical details that go with them. **¶5A(3)**: funds received are applied "**first to the option fee, then to the earnest money, and then to the additional earnest money**" — so a combined payment that is short leaves the earnest money underfunded rather than the option fee. And **¶5A(4)**: the buyer authorizes the escrow agent to release the option fee to the seller "at any time without further notice to or consent from Buyer," and "**the option fee will be credited to the Sales Price at closing**."

## The weekend rule is narrower than people think

**¶5A(2)** provides that if the last day to deliver the earnest money, the option fee or the additional earnest money falls on a **Saturday, Sunday or Legal Holiday**, the time is extended to the end of the next day that is not one of those. The form defines Legal Holiday by statute: a legal holiday described in **Sections 662.003(a) and 662.003(b)(4) and (6), Texas Government Code**.

Note what that rule covers. It is written into Paragraph 5, about delivery of those funds. It is not a general "all deadlines roll to Monday" rule for the whole contract. The option period itself runs on calendar days and expires at 5:00 p.m. on its final day.

Different clocks in the same contract: calendar days for the option period, the 5A(2) rollover for fund delivery, business days for the HOA's 10-day response, and "the earlier of the Closing Date or X days" for title objections. Mixing them up produces a confident wrong answer.

## What actually goes wrong

**Backdating to "make the numbers work."** Someone writes an earlier date so the option period lines up with a weekend, or because the offer was made days earlier. That is not a scheduling convenience. It is a misstatement on an executed contract, it can start deadlines that have already run, and it can hand a party a defense they would not otherwise have.

**Guessing forward.** Someone fills in the date the seller *plans* to sign. If the seller signs later, the contract now says deadlines began before it existed.

**Leaving it blank.** A contract circulating with no effective date has no computable deadlines. Nobody knows when the option fee is due, which means it gets delivered late, which means the buyer loses the unrestricted termination right entirely — the single most common way a Texas buyer forfeits protection they paid for.

**A multi-party signature that never got delivered.** Two sellers, one signs, the other's page is signed but never sent. No delivery, no effective date, and everyone proceeds as though the clock is running.

## If the date on the contract is wrong

Do not fix it by writing over it. Changing a date on an executed contract is changing a contract term, and Texas contracts are changed one way: an **Amendment to Contract, form 39-11, effective 07/01/2026**, signed by every party and delivered ([TREC contract forms](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts)).

If deadlines have already run against the wrong date, the situation is no longer a clerical question and the parties' rights may already have been affected. That is a question for a **Texas real estate attorney**, not something to solve by agreement in a group text.

## The habit that prevents all of it

The moment the contract goes effective, build the deadline calendar from that date and send it to everyone — buyer, seller, lender, title company, inspector.

- Option fee delivered to the title company, and confirmation from the title company that it was received.
- Option period expiration, with the time: 5:00 p.m. local.
- Financing approval deadline.
- Title commitment review and objection deadline.
- HOA document request date, and the 10-business-day outside date.
- Closing date.

The contract for an ordinary resale is **form 20-19, effective 07/01/2026** ([TREC](https://www.trec.texas.gov/forms/one-four-family-residential-contract-resale)); condominiums, builder new construction and farm and ranch use their own promulgated contracts, and the day counts live in the same place on each.

Every deadline in that list is enforceable against a party who misses it, and none of them send a reminder.
