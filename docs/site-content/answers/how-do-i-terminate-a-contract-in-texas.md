# How do I terminate a real estate contract in Texas?

You terminate a Texas contract by exercising a right the contract gives you and delivering written notice — most commonly during the option period using TREC form 38-8. Terminating the contract and getting the earnest money back are two separate events: escrowed funds cannot be released without mutual agreement or a court order.

## The rule underneath all of it

A signed contract is binding. You do not get out of it because you changed your mind. You get out of it because the contract, or a statute, gave you a right to get out of it — and you exercised that right the way it says to, before it expired.

So the first question is never "how do I cancel." It is "which right do I have, and when does it die."

## Route one: the option period

This is the clean exit, and it is the one most terminations use.

Paragraph 5B of the contract grants the buyer "the **unrestricted right to terminate** this contract by giving notice of termination to Seller within [blank] days after the Effective Date of this contract (Option Period)," and specifies that "Notices under this paragraph must be given **by 5:00 p.m. (local time where the Property is located)** by the date specified" ([TREC form 20-19, ¶5B](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf)). No justification required, no inspection report needed, no argument with the seller.

Paragraph 5B also states the outcome: "(i) **the option fee will not be refunded** and Escrow Agent shall release any option fee remaining with Escrow Agent to Seller; and (ii) **any earnest money will be refunded to Buyer**."

TREC publishes the form for the notice: **Notice of Buyer's Termination of Contract, form 38-8, effective 04/01/2025** ([TREC contract forms](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts)).

Days are counted as **calendar days**, with the effective date as day zero. A ten-day option period on a contract with a November 1 effective date expires at 5:00 p.m. on November 11.

Three things kill this route, and ¶5D names the first two:

> "If **no dollar amount is stated** as the option fee or if Buyer fails to deliver the option fee within the time required, **Buyer shall not have the unrestricted right to terminate** this contract under this paragraph 5."

**Late option fee delivery.** Paragraph 5A requires delivery to the Escrow Agent — the title company, not the seller — within **3 days after the Effective Date**. Paragraph 5A(2) extends that only if the last day is a Saturday, Sunday or Legal Holiday, defined by reference to Texas Government Code Sections 662.003(a) and 662.003(b)(4) and (6). Paragraph 5E: "**Time is of the essence** for this paragraph and strict compliance with the time for performance is required."

**A blank option fee.** Nothing to do with timing. A contract with an option period filled in but the option fee dollar blank left empty confers no unrestricted right to terminate at all. Read your own executed ¶5A and confirm a dollar figure is in it.

**The 5:00 p.m. deadline.** Not a soft deadline. An inspector who could not get out until the following Tuesday does not extend it. If more time is needed, the option period is extended in writing on the **Amendment to Contract, form 39-11, effective 07/01/2026**, signed by both parties before the current period expires ([TREC contract forms](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts)).

## Route two: the financing addendum

Once the option period ends, the financing exit is usually the most important one left standing.

The **Third Party Financing Addendum, form 40-11, effective 01/03/2025** governs it ([TREC contract forms](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts)). It creates a right to terminate tied to the buyer's ability to obtain approval for the financing described in the addendum, with its own deadline running from the effective date.

The failure mode is specific and it is common: the buyer assumes "my loan fell through" is self-executing. It is not. The right has a deadline, and it requires written notice. A buyer whose loan dies after the addendum's deadline has passed is in a contract with no financing exit and earnest money exposed.

Related exits with their own forms and their own clocks:

- **Addendum Concerning Right to Terminate Due to Lender's Appraisal, form 49-1** — the low-appraisal exit.
- **Addendum for Sale of Other Property by Buyer, form 10-6** — the home-sale contingency.
- **Addendum for Property Subject to Mandatory Membership in a Property Owners Association, form 36-11, effective 07/01/2026** — HOA subdivision information and resale certificate.

There is one more financing-adjacent trap worth naming. If the buyer elected to obtain the survey under **¶6C(2)** and then failed to, the contract says the buyer "**may not terminate** the contract under Paragraph 2B of the Third Party Financing Addendum because the survey was not obtained" ([TREC form 20-19, ¶6C(2)](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf)). A forgotten survey order can close the financing exit.

Read the deadline on every addendum attached to your contract the day it is signed, not the week it expires.

## Route three: the seller's disclosure right under §5.008 and ¶7B

This one surprises people because it can outlive the option period entirely, and the contract version is stronger than most buyers realize.

Texas Property Code §5.008 requires a seller of residential real property of **not more than one dwelling unit** to give the buyer a written notice of the property's condition ([Texas Property Code §5.008](https://codes.findlaw.com/tx/property-code/prop-sect-5-008/), [statute text](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._prop._code_section_5.008)).

Paragraph 7B of the contract implements it as a **check one box only** election, and option (2) is the one that creates the right:

> "If Buyer does **not receive** the Seller's Disclosure Notice, **Buyer may terminate this contract at any time prior to the closing** and the earnest money will be refunded to Buyer. If Seller delivers the Seller's Disclosure Notice, **Buyer may terminate this contract for any reason within 7 days after Buyer receives** the Seller's Disclosure Notice **or prior to the closing, whichever first occurs**, and the earnest money will be refunded to Buyer."

([TREC form 20-19, ¶7B(2)](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf))

Two distinct rights in one paragraph. While the notice is outstanding, the buyer can terminate **at any time prior to closing** — an open-ended exit with earnest money refunded. Once it is delivered, that collapses into **7 days after receipt, or prior to closing, whichever comes first**.

"For any reason" is the same unrestricted standard as the option period, and the seven days run from **receipt**, not from the effective date. A seller who delivers the disclosure two weeks into the contract has just handed the buyer a fresh seven-day window. A seller who never delivers it has left the exit open until the closing table.

Option (3) of ¶7B is the exemption box — the seller is not required to furnish the notice under the Texas Property Code. The statutory exemptions include transfers pursuant to court order, foreclosure, a trustee in bankruptcy, transfers between co-owners, transfers to a spouse or a descendant, transfers by a fiduciary administering an estate, and new residences not previously occupied. Exemption from the form is not a license to misrepresent the property.

The current TREC version of the notice is **Seller's Disclosure Notice, form 55-1, effective 05/28/2026** ([TREC contract forms](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts)).

## Route four: title — ¶6B and ¶6D

Two title-side exits, both easy to miss because they run on the title company's timeline rather than the buyer's.

**The commitment never arrives.** Paragraph 6B requires the seller to furnish the title commitment "**within 20 days after the Title Company receives a copy of this contract**." If it is late, delivery is "automatically extended up to **15 days or 3 days before the Closing Date, whichever is earlier**." If it still has not been delivered within the time required, "**Buyer may terminate this contract and the earnest money will be refunded to Buyer**" ([TREC form 20-19, ¶6B](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf)).

**Objections are not cured.** Under ¶6D the buyer may object in writing to defects, exceptions or encumbrances disclosed on the survey or in the commitment, by the earlier of the closing date or a stated number of days after receiving the commitment, exception documents and survey. Then:

- Failure to object in time "**will constitute a waiver** of Buyer's right to object," except that Schedule C requirements are not waived.
- Provided the seller is not obligated to incur expense, the seller cures timely objections within a **15-day Cure Period**, with the closing date extended as necessary.
- If the objections are not cured, the buyer has **5 days after the end of the Cure Period** to terminate with the earnest money refunded, or to waive. **Silence is a waiver.**

That last line is the whole point. The 5-day window is not a grace period — it is a decision deadline, and the default outcome of doing nothing is that the buyer takes the property with the defect.

A revised commitment, revised survey or new exception document restarts the same objection window as to the new matter.

## Route five: casualty — ¶14

If the property is damaged or destroyed by fire or other casualty after the effective date, ¶14 requires the seller to restore it "as soon as reasonably possible, but in any event by the Closing Date."

If the seller fails to do so due to factors beyond the seller's control, the buyer may (a) **terminate and have the earnest money refunded**, (b) extend the time for performance up to **15 days**, or (c) accept the property in its damaged condition with an assignment of insurance proceeds if the carrier permits, plus **a credit from the seller at closing in the amount of the deductible** ([TREC form 20-19, ¶14](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf)).

## Route six: the seller terminates

Sellers have far fewer exits. TREC publishes a **Notice of Seller's Termination of Contract, form 50-0**, but the form does not create a right — it papers one the contract already gave the seller ([TREC contract forms](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts)).

The two that exist in the base form:

**Late earnest money, under ¶5C.** If the buyer fails to timely deliver the earnest money, the seller may terminate the contract, exercise the remedies under ¶15, or both — but only "by providing notice to Buyer **before Buyer delivers the earnest money**" ([TREC form 20-19, ¶5C](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf)). That is a narrow window. A seller who sits on it until the wire lands has lost it.

**Buyer default, under ¶15.** The seller may "(a) enforce **specific performance**, seek such other relief as may be provided by law, or both, or (b) terminate this contract and **receive the earnest money as liquidated damages**, thereby releasing both parties from this contract."

A seller who simply wants out because a better offer arrived does not have a termination right, and acting as though they do creates real exposure. That conversation belongs with a Texas real estate attorney before anything is sent.

## Route seven: both parties agree

Anything the parties both sign is enforceable between them. A mutual termination and release, signed by buyer and seller, ends the contract and directs where the earnest money goes. This is how most messy situations actually resolve, and it is the fastest path when both sides want out.

## Terminating and getting your money back are two different things

This is the part that generates almost all of the frustration.

Termination is unilateral when you hold the right. Notice goes out, the contract ends.

**Release of escrowed earnest money is not unilateral.** It requires both parties to sign a release, or a court order ([Texas Real Estate Research Center](https://trerc.tamu.edu/article/option-period-basics-2360/)). The title company is neutral. It does not adjudicate who was right. If the seller declines to sign — out of disagreement, or out of pique — the money sits in escrow while the contract is already dead.

The option fee behaves differently and worse for the buyer. Under ¶5A(4) the buyer authorizes the escrow agent to release it to the seller "at any time without further notice to or consent from Buyer" ([TREC form 20-19, ¶5A(4)](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf)). It may already be gone.

The practical consequence is that your leverage is the quality of your paperwork. A termination that was timely, in writing, on the right form, delivered the way the contract requires, and documented, leaves a refusing seller with nothing to stand on. A sloppy one gives them an argument, and an argument is all it takes to freeze the money.

If a release stalls, the next step is a Texas real estate attorney and the contract's dispute provisions. There is no version of this where the title company breaks the tie for you.

## A short checklist before you send anything

1. Identify the exact right you are exercising — ¶5B option period, the Third Party Financing Addendum, the appraisal addendum, ¶7B(2) for the seller's disclosure, ¶6B or ¶6D on title, ¶14 casualty, or mutual agreement.
2. Confirm the deadline for that right, counted from the correct effective date.
3. Use the correct promulgated form.
4. Deliver it the way the contract specifies delivery, to the party it specifies, before the time it specifies — and for ¶5B, before 5:00 p.m. local time where the property is located.
5. Keep proof of delivery.
6. Send the earnest money release request as a separate, parallel action — and expect it to take longer.

The contract this all runs on is the **One to Four Family Residential Contract (Resale), TREC form 20-19, effective 07/01/2026** — 12 pages, footer-dated 05-04-2026 ([TREC form 20-19 PDF](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/20-19_4.pdf), [TREC form page](https://www.trec.texas.gov/forms/one-four-family-residential-contract-resale)) — or the corresponding promulgated form for a condo, new home, land or farm and ranch transaction, where the paragraph numbers differ.

One last thing worth saying plainly: ¶23 of the contract is titled **CONSULT AN ATTORNEY BEFORE SIGNING** and states that "TREC rules prohibit real estate brokers and sales agents from giving legal advice." Your agent can tell you what the deadline is and send the form. Whether you have a right to terminate in a contested situation is a legal question, and it goes to a Texas real estate attorney.
