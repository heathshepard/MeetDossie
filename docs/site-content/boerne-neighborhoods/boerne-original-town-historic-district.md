# Boerne Original Town and the Historic District

This is the oldest part of Boerne — the platted town grid around Main Street, with streets named Blanco, Cibolo, Evergreen, Frederick, Hosack, Johns, Live Oak and Lohmann. It is the only part of the area where you can genuinely walk to a restaurant, and it is the only part governed by historic design review rather than by a homeowners association.

## The numbers

Kendall County Appraisal District parcel records show **114 parcels in the Boerne Original Town subdivision covering roughly 106 acres**, with **94 improved** (Kendall CAD GIS parcel layer, retrieved September 2026). Note that "Original Town" is a specific platted subdivision; the City's Historic District overlay is a separate boundary that covers a broader stretch of the Main Street corridor.

Lots vary enormously because this is a 19th-century town grid that has been divided and consolidated for 150 years: 10th percentile about **0.107 acres**, median **0.362 acres**, 90th percentile about **1.77 acres**.

Values vary just as widely — **median market value near $677,000**, but the 10th percentile is around $250,000 and the 90th around $2,415,000. That ten-fold spread is real. Original Town contains small cottages, substantial historic homes, and commercial buildings on the same tax roll. Appraisal-district values, not asking prices, and here more than anywhere else the appraised figure may diverge sharply from what a restored property actually trades for.

## The Historic District — what it actually requires

The City of Boerne established its **Historic District in 1985**. The district runs along Main Street between Frederick Street and Oak Park Drive, a little over a mile — the stretch marketed as the Hill Country Mile — and encompasses **over 150 properties** (City of Boerne).

**There is no HOA here.** That is genuinely good news for monthly cost. But it is not the same as no rules, and buyers who hear "no HOA" and relax are making a mistake.

The **Historic Landmark Commission** reviews permits for the "construction, reconstruction, alteration, restoration, relocation, demolition, or razing" of a structure in a designated historic district. That review results in a **Certificate of Appropriateness**, and you need one before the work proceeds.

Practical consequences:

- Exterior changes are a city approval process with a meeting calendar, not a weekend decision.
- Applications go through the City's SmartGov portal, and submittals are due to the Planning Department **a minimum of 14 days before the meeting date**.
- The Commission is a 7-member body appointed by the mayor with Council approval, serving four-year terms.
- To qualify for historic designation, a property generally must be **more than 50 years old**.

The City publishes Historic District Guidelines that spell out what specifically triggers review — paint, windows, fencing, signage, additions. **Read that document before you buy if you have any renovation plans at all.** We were not able to extract its detailed trigger list, and the City's own pages give inconsistent information about when the Commission meets, so call Planning at (830) 248-1501 and confirm both the guidelines and the meeting schedule rather than relying on a website.

The distinction that matters: an HOA is a private contract enforced by a board. Historic district review is a **city ordinance enforced by the City**. You cannot vote it away, and it runs with the property.

## Housing stock

The oldest housing stock in the county. Individually listed National Register properties within or adjacent to the district include the **Kendall County Courthouse** and the **Joseph Dienger Building** on North Main; the **Kendall Inn** on West Blanco is another landmark of the period. Residential stock runs from modest frame cottages to substantial two-story homes, with limestone construction reflecting the town's German settlement history.

We were not able to verify a specific architectural style inventory from the Texas Historical Commission or National Register nomination documents, so we are not going to characterize the prevailing styles beyond what the City itself documents. If period architecture is the reason you are buying, get the actual nomination paperwork for the specific property.

Expect the usual older-home realities: foundations that have moved, original wiring somewhere in the walls, plumbing of uncertain vintage, and possible lead paint and asbestos in pre-1978 construction. Budget for a specialist inspection, not a standard one.

## Jurisdiction

Kendall CAD shows the **City of Boerne** on every Original Town parcel. Obviously — this is the city. City utilities, Boerne PD, city trash, city tax.

## Schools

**Boerne ISD.** A point in Original Town returns **Curington Elementary, Boerne Middle School North, and Boerne High School** through Boerne ISD's InfoFinder lookup (queried September 2026). Confirm your specific address; boundaries were redrawn in August 2024.

## Location

This is the one place in Kendall County where "walkable" is a fair description. Restaurants, the Kendall County Courthouse square, shops, the library, and Cibolo Creek are all on foot. I-10 is a few minutes away.

The tradeoff is that you live in the part of town everyone else drives to. Event weekends, the Christmas lighting, Market Days, and Dickens on Main bring traffic and parked cars to your street. Some owners love it. Some list within two years.

## Who it suits

It suits someone who genuinely wants to walk to dinner, who values period character, and who is willing to work inside a design review process. It suits a buyer with the patience and budget to restore rather than renovate.

It does not suit someone who wants a garage, a big yard, a quiet street on a festival weekend, or the freedom to replace the windows next month without asking anyone. And it does not suit a buyer who hears "no HOA" and assumes that means no oversight — here, the oversight is the City, and it has more authority than an HOA would.
